Par stock

Seven items on the shelf. Everything above is built from these.

SurfactantCastile soapLiquid, unscented. Not dish detergent — see below.
AcidWhite vinegar, 5%Grocery strength only. Never the 20–30% "horticultural" kind.
OxidizerHydrogen peroxide, 3%Brown bottle. Breaks down to water and oxygen — no rinse residue.
SolventIsopropyl alcohol, 70%70% outperforms 91% for sanitizing. The water is the point.
OilHorticultural or neem oilRefined, labeled for plants. Not motor or cooking oil.
MineralPotassium bicarbonatePowder. Fungal suppression and pH work.
MineralFinished compostThe one input with no bottled substitute.
Soap is not detergent. Dawn and its relatives are engineered degreasers — they strip the leaf's waxy cuticle along with the pest and burn foliage. True soap is a potassium or sodium salt of a fatty acid. Read the label: if it says "detergent," it's the wrong slot.

Never combine

  • Bleach + ammonia → chloramine gas. This is why there is no bleach on the par stock at all. Peroxide does the oxidizer job without the pairing risk.
  • Bleach + vinegar (or any acid) → chlorine gas. Same reason.
  • Peroxide + vinegar, stored together → peracetic acid. Fine as a fleeting rinse in industry, corrosive and irritating when a student mixes a bottle of it in a shed. Use one, rinse, then the other.
  • Oil + sulfur, within 30 days → phytotoxic burn. If anything sulfur-based has been applied, oil waits a month.
  • Anything + "a little more won't hurt" → the most common cause of leaf burn in this garden. Concentration is the recipe. Measure it.

Before anyone sprays

Four habits that matter more than which bottle you picked.

  1. Test one leaf first. Spray a single leaf, wait 24 hours, check for burn. Every plant reacts differently and this garden grows dozens of species.
  2. Never in sun or heat. Early morning or evening, under 85 °F. Soap and oil in full sun cook the leaf.
  3. Identify before you treat. Most of what looks like a pest problem is a water problem. Spraying a drought-stressed plant makes it worse.
  4. Label the bottle. Contents, concentration, date mixed, who mixed it. An unlabeled sprayer gets emptied, not guessed at.
A note on the word "organic." These mixes are low-toxicity and homemade — that is not the same as OMRI-listed or certified organic. Describe them accurately to visitors. And because Lancer Farms is a university operation, anything applied as a pesticide falls under California DPR rules regardless of whether it came from a pantry. Check with the county ag commissioner before students mix and apply.
